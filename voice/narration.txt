On the thirtieth of June, nineteen-oh-eight, the sky exploded over Siberia — and nothing ever hit the ground. Just taiga: thin pines, a slow river, Evenki herders and their reindeer. Then a light brighter than the sun, and a sound like gunfire — with no gun. A rock the size of a building hit the atmosphere at fifteen kilometers a second. At that speed, air cannot get out of the way. It squeezes until the rock gives up — exploding five kilometers above the forest. The blast wave slammed down and flattened everything. Millions of trees, knocked over like grass. Tents thrown into the air. The shockwave circled the planet. Twice. And directly underneath? Trees still standing. Stripped bare — but standing. Because the force came from straight above. A straw holds if you push end on, and folds if you push sideways. So does a tree. The blast blew the outer forest sideways. The middle, it only pressed. No crater. No rock. Scientists took almost twenty years just to reach the site. Now June thirtieth is International Asteroid Day — and NASA has a whole office watching the sky. That morning, a city-sized blast hit the emptiest place on Earth. It will not always be this lucky.
