# Research — The Tanganyika Laughter Epidemic (1962)

Mode: **web-verified** (2026-11-02). The narration was owner-approved and fact-checked before this package; the research
below re-checks every claim and records the sources. Pages marked *read* were opened this session; *snippet* = seen in search.

## What happened (consensus)
- **30 January 1962**: at a mission-run girls' boarding school in **Kashasha**, on the western shore of Lake Victoria near
  **Bukoba** (Bukoba district, Tanganyika), three girls began laughing and could not stop. *(Wikipedia read; Scibabe, Owaahh snippets)*
- **95 of 159 pupils** (aged 12-18) were affected; teachers were not. Attacks: laughing, crying, restlessness, some fainting;
  a fit lasted minutes to hours and recurred; symptoms lasted from a few hours up to 16 days. *(Wikipedia read, from Rankin & Philip 1963)*
- **18 March 1962** the school was closed and the girls sent home; it reopened 21 May and 57 more pupils were affected; closed again end of June.
- The girls carried it home: **Nshamba** (Muleba district, ~55 miles west of Bukoba) had 217 cases, mostly young villagers,
  over 34 days in April-May 1962; Ramashenye girls' middle school (48 girls) and other villages/schools followed.
- In total **14 schools** closed and **about 1,000 people** were affected, all within ~100 miles of Bukoba. *(Wikipedia read; Scibabe snippet)*
- It died out about **18 months** after it began (narration: "over a year"); some popular accounts stretch it to 1964 — not used.
- Doctors found **no toxic or infectious cause**; Rankin & Philip and later reviewers excluded poisoning and encephalitis.
- Independence: Tanganyika became independent on **9 December 1961**, seven weeks before the outbreak.

## Why (theories; stated as "the leading explanation", not settled)
- **Mass psychogenic illness** under stress: linguist **Christian Hempelmann** (Humor, 2007) links it to the strain of the
  months after independence and the pressure on pupils from strict teachers and parents' expectations. *(Wikipedia read; Hempelmann PDF snippet)*
- **Robert Provine** (laughter researcher, *Laughter: A Scientific Investigation*, American Scientist 1996) popularised the
  case as contagious laughter; interviewed on it by Lawrence Weschler. *(snippets)*
- Bartholomew & Wessely: a culture-specific conversion reaction (conflict between conservative home life and new school ideas).
- **No joke**: no account records a joke or a funny trigger; the laughter came with crying and distress. *(all sources)*
- Caveat: Hempelmann argues popular retellings exaggerate; historians criticise colonial-era "hysteria" framing — the video
  treats the girls as distressed patients, never as a punchline.
