- **C01** (fact, high): In July 1518 a woman in Strasbourg (then in the Holy Roman Empire) began dancing alone in the street; later accounts name her Frau Troffea. — qualification: do not name her as fact
- **C02** (fact, medium): She kept dancing for days (about a week by most accounts).
- **C03** (fact, high): Within days (about a week) more than thirty people had joined her.
- **C04** (disputed, medium): How many danced is disputed: the city clerk Sebastian Brant wrote of about fifty; the later Imlin family chronicle says four hundred. — qualification: say 'dozens'; never a total
- **C05** (fact, high): Physicians blamed 'overheated blood' and prescribed more dancing so the afflicted could dance it out.
- **C06** (fact, high): The council had platforms (stages) built in the horse market and the grain market, opened guild halls and paid musicians to keep the dancers going.
- **C07** (fact, high): The approach backfired: more people joined, and the council then banned public music and dancing.
- **C08** (disputed, low): Later chronicles claim that at the peak up to fifteen people died each day. — qualification: only as 'later chronicles claim', followed by the city records
- **C09** (fact, medium): Strasbourg's own records from 1518 do not mention any deaths. — qualification: 'the city's own records mention none'

**Description**
```
In July 1518, in Strasbourg, a woman stepped into the street and began to dance. She kept going for days. Within about a week more than thirty people had joined her, and the outbreak grew through the summer. The city's physicians blamed "overheated blood" and prescribed more dancing, so the council opened guild halls, set up stages in the horse market and the grain market, and paid musicians to keep the dancers going. It backfired: more people joined, and the council then banned public music and dancing and sent the worst-affected to the shrine of Saint Vitus at Saverne, in red shoes. By early September it was over.

What is disputed: how many danced (the city clerk Sebastian Brant wrote of about fifty; a later chronicle says four hundred) and whether anyone died. Later chronicles claim up to fifteen deaths a day at the peak, but Strasbourg's own records from 1518 do not mention any deaths. The name "Frau Troffea" comes from later accounts.

Why? The leading explanation, from historian John Waller, is mass psychogenic illness: a population worn down by years of famine, disease and the highest grain prices in a generation, who believed Saint Vitus could curse sinners with dancing. Ergot poisoning is widely rejected; some scholars read it as a religious ritual instead.
```
